H: Good evening, everyone. When I started the Kilbride Memory Project four years ago, I had no idea what I was letting myself in for. The idea was simple: to record older people in the village talking about their lives before the stories disappeared. So far, we've recorded sixty-three people, and the oldest was a hundred and two.
H: The first thing I learnt was that people rarely tell you what you expect. I'd assumed most of them would want to talk about the war, or about hardship. Instead, again and again, they told me about dancing. One woman, Nora, insisted that the Saturday dances in the parish hall had been the most important thing in her life. More important, she said, than her wedding. She made me promise not to tell her daughter she'd said that.
H: Another man, Pat, who'd been a postman for forty years, claimed he knew every secret in the village because he'd read the backs of all the postcards. When I asked whether he'd ever told anyone, he denied having breathed a word, and then he spent the next hour telling me everything.
H: Recording people turned out to be a skill in itself. At the beginning, I kept interrupting and asking people to explain what they meant. An old farmer called Michael eventually told me, quite kindly, that if I stopped asking questions, he might actually get round to answering one. He was right. Now I ask one question, and then I keep quiet, sometimes for twenty minutes.
H: People often ask how accurate the memories are. Honestly, some of them aren't. Two brothers described the same flood in nineteen fifty-four. One said the water had reached the church steps, while the other swore it had come up to the windows of the pub. I don't try to decide who was right. What I'm recording isn't the past, exactly. It's how people remember the past, and that's interesting in itself.
H: The hardest part has been the endings. Eleven of the people we recorded have since died, and their families have often told me that the recording is the only time they ever heard their parent talk about their childhood. One son said it was like meeting his father for the first time.
H: One of the nicest surprises has been the local primary school. Every term, a class of ten-year-olds comes to the library to interview one of our older volunteers. The children prepare their own questions, and I can tell you they're far less polite than I am. One boy asked a retired teacher whether she'd ever been sent to the headmaster herself. She admitted that she had, twice.
H: All the recordings are now kept in the county library, and anyone can listen to them free of charge. We've also just published a small book of extracts, and every copy sold helps to pay for the equipment.
H: If you'd like to get involved, we're particularly looking for volunteers who can type up the interviews. You don't need any experience, just patience and a good ear. My only warning is that once you start listening, you'll find it very hard to stop.
