A: My guest today is Marcus Bell, who has worked as a conference interpreter for over twenty years, translating between English, French and Spanish. Marcus, a lot of people assume interpreting is just translating very fast. Is that fair?
F: It's a common assumption, and I can see why people make it. But no. When I started, my trainer warned me that if I tried to translate every word, I'd drown within ten minutes. What you're really doing is reporting what someone means, a fraction of a second after they've said it. You're constantly asking yourself not "What did she say?" but "What is she trying to tell them?"
A: Do speakers ever make that harder?
F: Constantly. The worst are people who read from a script at top speed. Once, a minister apologised to the audience for running late and announced that he would skip the introduction, and then he read the rest twice as fast to make up for it. Afterwards, one of the delegates came up and congratulated us on keeping up. We hadn't, entirely, but it was kind of him to say so.
A: What about jokes?
F: Oh, jokes are a nightmare. Humour rarely survives translation. A colleague of mine once admitted that she'd simply told the audience, in Spanish, "The speaker has just told a joke. It doesn't translate, but he'd be grateful if you laughed." And they did, right on cue. The speaker was delighted. He told her afterwards that he'd never had such a good reaction.
A: Is it true that interpreters always work in pairs?
F: For anything longer than half an hour, yes. We take turns, usually every thirty minutes, because concentration drops off sharply after that. The one who isn't speaking isn't resting, though. They're writing down numbers and names for their partner, checking terminology, passing notes. People are often surprised to hear that the silent interpreter is working almost as hard as the one at the microphone.
A: Is there ever pressure to soften what someone says?
F: Very much so, and it's the part of the job I find hardest. Some years ago, a delegate accused another country of lying, in fairly undiplomatic language. His own team asked me afterwards whether I could have chosen a milder word. I told them that wasn't my decision to make. My job is to report what was said, not to improve it. If a speaker is rude, the audience has a right to know that.
A: Has technology changed things?
F: Enormously. Machine translation is remarkably good now for written texts, and people regularly ask me whether I'm worried about losing my job. I'm not, or not yet. Machines are good at words, but they're still poor at the things that matter most in a live room: tone, irony, the moment when someone says "yes" but clearly means "absolutely not". Mind you, I said much the same thing ten years ago about written translation, and look what happened there.
A: And finally, what advice would you give someone who's thinking of becoming an interpreter?
F: Read widely, far beyond your own field. On any given day, I might be interpreting a debate on fishing rights in the morning and a conference on heart surgery in the afternoon. The interpreters who last aren't necessarily the best linguists. They're the ones who are curious about everything. Oh, and look after your voice. Nobody tells you that at the start.
A: Marcus Bell, thank you very much.
