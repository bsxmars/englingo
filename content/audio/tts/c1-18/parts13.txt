A: Part three. You will hear an interview with a wildlife photographer, Mia Torres.
D: My guest today is the wildlife photographer Mia Torres, whose pictures of snow leopards have appeared in magazines all over the world. Mia, you've been doing this for nearly twenty years. How did it begin?
G: Well, I'd been working as a lab technician for about five years, and I'd been taking photos on the side, mostly birds in the park near my flat. Then one of them won a small competition, and I suppose that gave me the courage to give up the day job. I've been freelance ever since.
D: Your best-known work comes from the Himalayas. What's it actually like, waiting for a snow leopard?
G: Mostly cold and boring! People imagine you just turn up and there it is. The first time, I spent eleven weeks in a hide and saw one on just two occasions, both a long way off. The second trip, I'd hardly set up the camera on the fourth morning when a female walked straight into the frame, as if she'd been waiting for me. Pure luck, mostly.
D: Do you ever feel you're disturbing the animals?
G: All the time, and I think anyone who says they don't isn't being honest. I have a rule now: if an animal changes its behaviour because of me, I back off. Had I followed that rule early in my career, I'd have fewer dramatic photos, but I'd feel a lot better about them.
D: There's been a lot of debate about digitally altered images in competitions. Where do you stand?
G: I'm fairly strict. Adjusting the light is one thing; removing a branch, or adding one, is quite another. A few years ago a competition had to disqualify a photographer whose winning picture turned out to show a stuffed animal, which did enormous damage to everyone's credibility, not just his.
D: You also run workshops for young photographers. What do you tell them?
G: To slow down. Most of them want the perfect shot on day one, and when they don't get it, they give up on the whole idea. I tell them to pick one place near home and keep going back to it. You learn far more from one park visited fifty times than from fifty parks visited once.
D: And what's next for you?
G: Something closer to home, actually. I've been asked to photograph urban foxes in London, and I've said I will. My family think I've lost my mind, swapping mountains for rubbish bins. Perhaps I have. But if someone's going to show that cities are full of wildlife, it may as well be me.
