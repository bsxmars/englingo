G: Today I'm talking to Owen Hale, who has led a volunteer mountain rescue team in the Scottish Highlands for twelve years. Owen, you must get asked this all the time. Why would anyone do this for nothing?
B: I do get asked, yes, and I'm never quite sure what people want me to say. I suppose the honest answer is that I can't imagine not doing it. I grew up in these hills. When I was nineteen, my uncle was caught out by the weather on a ridge not far from here, and if a team hadn't reached him that night, he might well not have survived. You don't forget something like that. Although I'd be lying if I said it was all noble motives. There's a certain satisfaction in being useful, in being the person who can actually do something.
G: Talk me through a typical call-out.
B: There isn't really a typical one, which is part of the appeal, I suppose. But the pattern is often the same. Someone sets off in the morning in lovely weather, in trainers, with a phone at forty per cent and no map, because they assume the phone will do the job. By mid-afternoon the cloud has come down, the battery's dead, and they've no idea which way they ought to go. Now, I'm not going to sit here and say those people should have stayed at home. Everyone has to start somewhere. But they could have saved themselves a great deal of trouble with about ten minutes of planning.
G: Do you find it frustrating?
B: Less than you might think. What does get to me is when people refuse to turn back. We had a group last winter, experienced walkers, who must have seen the forecast, because it had been on every news bulletin for two days, and they went up anyway. One of them broke an ankle near the summit, and getting him down took eleven of us nine hours. They needn't have taken that risk at all. The mountain would still have been there the following weekend.
G: There's been some debate recently about whether people should have to pay for their own rescue. Where do you stand?
B: Firmly against. I understand the argument. Why should a charity, or the taxpayer, foot the bill for someone else's carelessness? But the moment you charge for rescue, people start hesitating before they call. Someone thinks, well, I might be able to get down on my own, and they wait an extra two hours, and by then it's dark and they're hypothermic. I'd far rather be called out to ten people who turn out to be fine than arrive too late for one who wasn't.
G: How has the work changed since you started?
B: The technology, obviously. We can sometimes locate someone from their phone signal to within a few hundred metres, which twenty years ago would have been unthinkable. But there's a downside. People will now set off on routes they'd never have attempted before, because they assume that if things go wrong, someone will be able to find them. And usually we can. Usually.
G: And finally, what would you say to someone who's thinking of joining a team?
B: Come and talk to us, but be realistic. You'll be expected to train two evenings a month and at least one weekend, and you'll have to be prepared to leave a family dinner at a moment's notice. It isn't for everyone, and nobody will think any less of you if you decide it isn't for you. But if it is, I can promise you won't regret it.
G: Owen Hale, thank you.
