B: You will hear five short extracts in which people talk about changing career later in life.
B: Speaker one.
G: I'd been working as an accountant for nearly twenty years, and I wouldn't have said I was unhappy. Then, at a friend's wedding, I was seated next to a woman who restored old boats for a living. We talked for about three hours, and by the end of the evening I'd more or less made up my mind. It still seems slightly absurd that one dinner should have changed everything. These days I spend my mornings sanding wood in a cold workshop, and what's caught me off guard is how much I love the sameness of it. I always used to think routine was the enemy. It turns out it was just the wrong routine.
B: Speaker two.
D: When the bank closed our regional office, I was offered a transfer to London, and I turned it down. My father had been unwell for some time, and I didn't want to be four hours away from him, so that more or less settled it. I retrained as a maths teacher, which at forty-six felt rather like going back to school myself. The salary is a fraction of what it was, and I won't pretend that hasn't been hard. But I'm about to finish my second year, and I've just been asked to run the department's exam preparation. Nobody had asked me to run anything for years. I'm rather pleased with myself, to be honest.
B: Speaker three.
E: I'd always assumed I would be a lawyer until I retired. It simply never occurred to me that there was a choice. Then I spent ten days in hospital after a cycling accident. Nothing life-threatening, as it turned out, but it gave me a lot of time to think, and to watch the nurses at work. I started my nursing degree eighteen months later. People expect me to say it's exhausting, and it is. But mostly I keep thinking about all those years I spent drafting contracts I didn't care about. If I'd been braver at twenty-five, I'd have been doing this for two decades by now.
B: Speaker four.
H: Our company was taken over, and within a year the new owners had turned a place I'd loved into one where nobody could make a decision without three meetings first. I'd been cooking for friends for years, so I signed up for a course at a catering college, partly out of spite, if I'm honest. Three years on, I'm a junior chef in a busy restaurant in Cork. I'm the oldest person in the kitchen by about fifteen years, and the head chef still has me peeling vegetables half the day. I know you've got to put the time in, but I'll be fifty next spring, and I've no intention of still peeling carrots at fifty-five.
B: Speaker five.
C: Ever since I was a child I'd wanted to work outdoors, and for years I kept telling myself I'd do it one day. In the end there was no single moment. I just ran out of reasons not to. I now lead walking tours in the Lake District. My friends think it's wonderfully romantic, and on a clear day in June it is. On a wet Tuesday in November, with a group who'd clearly rather be in the pub, I'm less sure. I don't regret it, exactly. I just haven't decided yet whether I've found the right life or simply a different one.
