B: Extract two. You hear a writer talking about his first novel.
E: Your first novel came out when you were fifty-three. Had you always intended to write?
D: Not at all. For most of my career I was an engineer, and I'd have laughed at anyone who suggested otherwise. Fiction seemed a slightly frivolous thing to me, if I'm honest. Something other people did at weekends.
E: So what changed?
D: Nothing dramatic. I was stuck in a hotel in Aberdeen for a week, because a storm had closed the airport, and I started writing down the stories my grandfather used to tell. By the end of the week I had forty pages, and I'd grown rather attached to them.
E: And the novel grew out of those forty pages?
D: Eventually. It took another six years. What surprised me was how useful engineering turned out to be. You learn that a structure has to bear weight, that every part has to earn its place. A chapter is no different, really.
E: Some critics have called the book old-fashioned.
D: Well, they're not wrong. It has a beginning, a middle and an end, in that order. I've come to think that's rather a radical choice these days.
B: Extract three. You hear two friends talking about a charity cycling event.
C: So this time next week you'll be cycling across the Pyrenees. Are you nervous?

C: But you've been training, haven't you? Every time I look out of the window, you seem to be heading off somewhere on that bike.
H: I have, but mostly on the flat. The organisers sent round the route last night, and the second day is nothing but climbing. I was about to write back and ask whether there's a bus option.
C: You were not!
H: I was, honestly. Then I remembered I'd already collected nearly two thousand euros from people at work, and they're hardly going to sponsor me to sit on a bus.
C: Well, I think you'll surprise yourself. And if it all goes wrong, you'll at least have a good story to tell.
H: That's what my wife said. Though she also said she'd be waiting at the finish line with a camera, which sounds more like a threat than a promise.
