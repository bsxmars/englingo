E: Today on Word of Mouth I'm talking to Ben Hartley, who has spent twenty years as a dictionary editor. Ben, what does your job actually involve?
B: Mostly reading, and listening. We have an enormous collection of real English, from newspapers, websites, even transcripts of radio programmes. When a new word starts appearing regularly, my team looks at how it's used. The decision to include it isn't based on whether we like it. It's based on evidence.
E: So how does a word earn its place?
B: It needs to be used widely, by different kinds of people, over a period of several years. A word that's extremely popular for one summer and then disappears is really just a fashion. We usually wait and see, and most of those never make it in.
E: Where do most new words come from these days?
B: People often imagine they're completely invented, but that's actually quite rare. The majority are built from parts we already have. Take a prefix like "un". Twenty years ago, nobody would have said they'd unfollowed someone. Now it's in every dictionary. Or think of suffixes. "Able" is incredibly productive. People talk about a bingeable series, or a walkable city.
E: Do you ever get complaints?
B: All the time! Some listeners write to say that adding words like that is a sign that the language is in decline. I understand the feeling, but English has always worked like this. Unhelpful and careless were new once, too. What I find more worrying is when people become unwilling to accept any change at all.
E: Is there a word you personally dislike?
B: Oh, impactful. I find it rather unattractive. But my job isn't to judge. If millions of people use it, it goes in.
E: Has technology made your work easier?
B: In some ways, enormously. We can search billions of words in seconds. But the final decisions still depend on human judgement. A computer can tell me how often a word appears, but not whether it's meant as a joke.
E: Finally, any advice for learners who come across a word they don't know?
B: Break it into pieces. If you know "employ", you can usually work out unemployment, employer, employable. That's how native speakers deal with unfamiliar words, too.
E: Ben Hartley, thank you.
