D: Good afternoon. My name's Richard Hale, and I've spent the last twenty-two years working as a lexicographer, which, for those of you who've never met one, means I write dictionaries for a living. I'm often asked how a word gets into the dictionary, as though there were a committee that votes on it. In reality, the process is a great deal less glamorous and, I'd argue, a great deal more democratic.
D: Our starting point is something called a corpus: an enormous collection of real English, currently around twenty billion words, taken from newspapers, novels, websites and recorded conversations. When a new word starts to crop up, the software flags it, and one of us takes a closer look.
D: What we're looking for is evidence of three things. First, frequency: is the word being used widely, or only by a handful of enthusiasts? Second, spread: does it appear in different kinds of sources, or is it confined to, say, technology blogs? And third, and this is the one people tend to overlook, staying power. As a rule of thumb, we want to see a word in steady use for at least five years before it earns a full entry. A great many words that seemed to be on the verge of becoming permanent simply vanish within a couple of years.
D: Of course, a dictionary isn't just a list of words. Much of my working day is spent on collocations, the words that a given word tends to keep company with. Learners, in particular, rely heavily on these. It's one thing to know what concern means; it's quite another to know that you raise a concern, voice a concern or address a concern, but you don't make one. Our corpus tells us, for example, that heavy goes with rain, traffic and smoker, but almost never with wind, where we say strong.
D: We also pay close attention to dependent prepositions, which change more often than you might expect. Thirty years ago, different to was frowned upon in some style guides; today our data show it's perfectly standard in British English, though different from remains the safer choice in formal writing. Meanwhile, bored of, which many of my older colleagues still object to, is now more common than bored with among speakers under thirty.
D: People sometimes accuse us of lowering standards by including informal words. I take the opposite view. A dictionary that ignored how people actually speak would be of little use to anyone. Our job isn't to pass judgement on the language; it's to describe it accurately, and that includes labelling words as informal, offensive or dated, so that readers can make their own choices.
D: The hardest part of the job, believe it or not, is writing definitions for the simplest words. The entry for the verb set, which has well over four hundred senses, took one of my colleagues the best part of nine months. By comparison, defining a technical term is a holiday.
