B: My guest today is Laura Kent, who has written fourteen autobiographies, none of them her own. Laura is a ghostwriter. Laura, how did you get into this?
E: Completely by chance. I was working as a sports journalist, and a footballer I'd interviewed rang me and said he'd been offered a book deal. He asked whether I'd help him write it. I told him I'd never written a book, and he said that was fine, because he'd never read one!
B: So what's the first thing you do with a new client?
E: I just listen. For the first few weeks I don't write anything at all. I ask them what their earliest memory is, who they were afraid of as a child, that kind of thing. People usually tell me they've got nothing interesting to say, and then three hours later they're still talking.
B: Do clients ever lie to you?
E: Oh, all the time. Though not always deliberately. One singer insisted that she'd grown up very poor. Then her brother told me they'd had a swimming pool in the garden. When I asked her about it, she admitted it had been, quote, quite a small pool.
B: Have you ever refused to work with someone?
E: Once. A businessman wanted me to say that he'd invented a product which, as far as I knew, his business partner had designed. I told him I wouldn't write it. He accused me of being disloyal, and we never spoke again.
B: Is it hard not to get any credit?
E: People always assume it must be. Honestly, I don't mind. My mother used to ask me when I was going to write a real book, and I'd tell her that I already had, fourteen times. What I do find hard is when a client reads the finished book and says it doesn't sound like them. Usually that means it sounds exactly like them, and they don't like it.
B: Any advice for someone who'd like to do your job?
E: Learn to keep quiet. My first editor warned me that a ghostwriter who talks about her clients won't have any clients for long. And she was absolutely right.
B: Laura Kent, thank you so much.
